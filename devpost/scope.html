Scope · Proof of concept

Noon Six (working title)

A tiny, hackable daily priority list. It stores your issues in plain text, and every day at noon it confronts you with the 6 it thinks matter most — and tells you why.

status: approved

The unique kernel

Most to-do apps store tasks and wait for you to decide. This one makes the decision for you — and then shows its work.

At noon it ranks your issues by how long they've been waiting (oldest first), then by urgency, then difficulty, then deadline, and it shows each issue's age so the decision is legible. The point isn't an organized list — it's trusting a decision so you stop second-guessing yourself. The accountability layer ("the one you keep not doing keeps climbing, visibly a day older") is what makes it more than a generic list.

How it works

daily loop everything lives in one editable text file

Who it's for

You, personally — someone who finds it hard to decide what to work on and tends to second-guess priorities, and who wants a daily nudge that feels like confidence rather than another pile of tasks. Others could use it, but they aren't the target.

Hackathon audience: a reviewer who should get, in about a minute, that the whole thing is meant to be opened up and edited.

Why it matters

Prioritizing has always been hard, and second-guessing gets in the way. You picture this app and "feel more confident and ready to get stuff done." You also want to understand what you make — to edit and grow it yourself — and to get real daily use from something you built.

The core loop

  • Add issues: title, description, difficulty (L/M/H), urgency (L/M/H), optional deadline, and an optional free-text Ranking Explanation — your own reason it matters, is hard, or is necessary.
  • Open it at/after noon — today's 6 are waiting, each with why it ranked there and how often it has come up undone.
  • Reorder by hand to break ties; your manual order and explanation survive the next noon re-rank.
  • Finish one, delete it — a count of what you deleted in the last two days sits above the list.
  • Come back tomorrow, because the list tells you what to do and remembers what you've been dodging.

What "working" looks like

Open the app at/after noon: 6 issues ranked and waiting, each with a short reason and its age. Add, complete, delete. Then the proving moment — add a Ranking Explanation and drag an issue to a new spot, let the next noon re-rank run, and confirm your order and explanation are still there while the automatic ranking updated around them. Edit an issue in the text file, change a code-level value (count, noon time), reload: the list reflects it and nothing you'd saved is lost.

The "oh, that's cool" beat: editing a plain text file changes how the app behaves, and everything already saved stays intact.

Now / Later / Cut

  • Local app; issues stored in a human-editable text file (no cloud, no database, no accounts).
  • Issues with title, description, difficulty, urgency, optional deadline, and an optional Ranking Explanation.
  • Top-6 daily list ranked: days since creation (oldest first) → urgency → difficulty → deadline (closest first).
  • A visible reason per rank, and its age shown on the row.
  • Manual reorder as a tie-breaker; the manual order and Ranking Explanation persist across re-ranks.
  • Complete and delete; count of items deleted in the last two days.
  • Code-level settings (easy to change in source): number of results, alert time, difficulty/urgency levels. Issue data lives in the text file. Re-rank reflects edits; data survives.
  • When open at noon it refreshes quietly; the update shows as the red badge (no pop-up, no background notification).
  • A true scheduled notification that fires while the app is closed.
  • Making it visibly nicer / more polished.
  • Syncing or backing up beyond the local text file.
  • Accounts, login, multi-user — a personal tool; cost and complexity with no payoff here.
  • Cloud storage or a database — would make the data harder to hand-edit, the opposite of the point.
  • Mobile app — a desktop/local app demonstrates the loop fine.
  • Notifications while closed — deferred (see Later); opening at noon keeps the build small.
  • Analytics, dashboards, collaboration — the wish to "add more and more" is exactly what a proof of concept is meant to resist.
